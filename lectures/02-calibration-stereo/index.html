Part 1

The full camera model

The camera is not at the origin

Points are given in a world frame $W$ (a table, a map, the robot base). To project $P$, first move it to the camera frame $C$:

$$x_c = \textcolor{#e7e9ee}{R}\,x_w + \textcolor{#f2b134}{t}, \qquad \text{then } K \text{ gives the pixel}$$

  • $R$: rows are the camera axes $x_c, y_c, z_c$ in world coordinates (arc: dashed world axes → camera axes)
  • $\textcolor{#f2b134}{t}$: the world origin seen from the camera, along the camera axes
  • camera centre $\textcolor{#b28dff}{C}$ maps to $0$: $\;t = -R\,C$ — not the camera position

Extrinsics $R, t$ (6 DoF): ${}^{C}T_{W} = \begin{bmatrix} R & t\\ 0^\top & 1\end{bmatrix}$; the camera pose in the world is ${}^{W}T_{C} = \begin{bmatrix} R^\top & C\\ 0^\top & 1\end{bmatrix}$.

Extrinsics to play with

The projection matrix $P$

$$\begin{bmatrix}\tilde u\\ \tilde v\\ \tilde w\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} f_x & s & o_x \\ 0 & f_y & o_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{K\ \text{(intrinsics)}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13} & t_x\\ r_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23} & t_y\\ r_{31} & r_{32} & r_{33} & t_z\end{bmatrix}}_{[R\,|\,t]\ \text{(extrinsics)}} \begin{bmatrix} x_w\\ y_w\\ z_w\\ 1\end{bmatrix} = P\,\tilde x_w, \qquad (u, v) = \Big(\frac{\tilde u}{\tilde w}, \frac{\tilde v}{\tilde w}\Big)$$

The projection matrix $P$: properties

  • $P = K[R\,|\,t]$ is $3\times4$
  • $P$ and $kP$ ($k \ne 0$) give the same pixels: defined up to scale
  • $\tilde w = $ depth $z_c$ of the point (when $P$ is normalised so that $\|(p_{31}, p_{32}, p_{33})\| = 1$)
  • the camera centre: $P\begin{bmatrix}C\\1\end{bmatrix} = K(RC + t) = 0$

Counting degrees of freedom

$P$: 12 entries, up to scale → 11 DoF.

$K$ with $f_x, f_y, o_x, o_y$: 4  +  $R$: 3  +  $t$: 3  =  10.

Which parameter is missing?

The skew $s$ in $K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & s & o_x \\ 0 & f_y & o_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$: pixel axes not perpendicular.

Every $P$ whose left $3\times3$ block is invertible splits uniquely into $K[R\,|\,t]$ with this $K$ (positive diagonal): $4 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 11$.

Skew in practice

$s \ne 0$ means the pixel axes are not perpendicular. Not the case for digital sensors (OpenCV fixes $s = 0$); it appears when an image is warped with a shear, or for film scanned with a slightly slanted scan direction. We keep $s$ so that every $P$ splits into $K[R\,|\,t]$.

Part 2

Camera calibration

Calibration: find $K$ (and the pose)

Why

  • pixel → ray needs $K^{-1}$: every 3D measurement (PnP, stereo, SLAM) needs $K$
  • lens distortion must be estimated as well
  • datasheet values are not precise enough (focus, assembly tolerances)

Procedure

  1. take a picture of an object of known geometry
  2. find correspondences: 3D points $X_i$ ↔ pixels $(u_i, v_i)$
  3. each correspondence gives equations on $P$
  4. solve for $P$, then split $P$ into $K$, $R$, $t$

Each correspondence gives two linear equations

Write $p_1^\top, p_2^\top, p_3^\top$ for the rows of $P$ and $\tilde X_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i, 1)$:

$$u_i = \frac{p_1^\top \tilde X_i}{p_3^\top \tilde X_i}, \qquad v_i = \frac{p_2^\top \tilde X_i}{p_3^\top \tilde X_i}$$

Multiply out the denominators — the equations become linear in the entries of $P$:

$$\begin{aligned} p_1^\top \tilde X_i - u_i\, p_3^\top \tilde X_i &= 0\\ p_2^\top \tilde X_i - v_i\, p_3^\top \tilde X_i &= 0\end{aligned}$$

Stack all $n$ points, unknown $p = (p_{11}, p_{12}, \dots, p_{34}) \in \mathbb{R}^{12}$:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \tilde X_1^\top & 0^\top & -u_1 \tilde X_1^\top\\ 0^\top & \tilde X_1^\top & -v_1 \tilde X_1^\top\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\ \tilde X_n^\top & 0^\top & -u_n \tilde X_n^\top\\ 0^\top & \tilde X_n^\top & -v_n \tilde X_n^\top \end{bmatrix}}_{A\ (2n\times12)} p = 0$$

11 unknowns, 2 equations per point → at least 6 points, not all on one plane.

Fixing the scale

$Ap = 0$ always has the useless solution $p = 0$, and if $p$ solves it, so does $kp$ — the same camera.

With noise there is no exact solution anyway. So we ask for:

$$\min_p \|Ap\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|p\|^2 = 1$$

Why not $p_{34} = 1$?

Another way to fix the scale, and then plain least squares. But $p_{34} = (Kt)_3 = t_z$: if the world origin happens to be close to the camera's $z_c = 0$ plane, $p_{34} \approx 0$ and fixing it to $1$ blows up the other entries. The constraint $\|p\| = 1$ has no such special case.

Constrained least squares

$$\|Ap\|^2 = (Ap)^\top(Ap) = p^\top \underbrace{A^\top A}_{M} \, p \qquad\text{— a quadratic form, } M \text{ symmetric } 12 \times 12$$

Lagrangian:  $\mathcal{L}(p, \lambda) = p^\top M p - \lambda\,(p^\top p - 1)$.  Setting its gradient to zero:

$$\nabla_p\, \mathcal{L} = 2Mp - 2\lambda p = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^\top A\, p = \lambda\, p$$

Every solution is a unit eigenvector of $A^\top A$. Two questions:

  1. where does $\nabla_p (p^\top M p) = 2Mp$ come from?
  2. why does the Lagrangian give the optimum, and which eigenvector?

The derivative of a quadratic form

$$q(p) = p^\top M p = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} M_{ij}\, p_i\, p_j$$

Differentiate with respect to $p_k$: the terms with $i = k$ give $\sum_j M_{kj} p_j$, the terms with $j = k$ give $\sum_i M_{ik} p_i$:

$$\frac{\partial q}{\partial p_k} = (Mp)_k + (M^\top p)_k$$

$$\nabla q = (M + M^\top)\,p = 2Mp \quad \text{for symmetric } M$$

Sanity check in 1D

$q(p) = m p^2$, $\;q'(p) = 2mp$.

Also useful

$\nabla_p (b^\top p) = b$,   $\nabla_p \|p\|^2 = 2p$.

So $\nabla_p \|Ap - b\|^2 = 2A^\top(Ap - b)$ — setting it to zero gives the normal equations $A^\top A p = A^\top b$ of ordinary least squares.

The Lagrangian method: intuition

Problem

$$\begin{aligned} \text{maximise}\quad & \textcolor{#f2b134}{f(x, y) = x^2 y} \\ \text{subject to}\quad & \textcolor{#5ab0ff}{g(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0} \end{aligned}$$

the constraint $g = 0$ is the unit circle

At the optimum the contour of $f$ touches the circle — if it crossed it, we could move along the circle to a higher contour. Touching = parallel normals:

$$\nabla f = \lambda\, \nabla g$$

Solutions: $(0, \pm1)$, $(\pm\sqrt{2/3}, \pm 1/\sqrt3)$.

Only candidates: evaluate $f$, pick the best.

Which eigenvector?

Candidates: unit eigenvectors, $A^\top A\,p = \lambda p$.

The loss at a candidate: $$\begin{aligned}\|Ap\|^2 &= p^\top A^\top A\, p\\ &= p^\top (\lambda p) = \lambda\end{aligned}$$

Pick the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of $A^\top A$.

In code: the last right singular vector of $A$,
np.linalg.svd(A)[2][-1]

From $P$ to $K$, $R$, $t$

$$P = K\,[R\,|\,t] = [\,KR \;|\; Kt\,]$$

  • left $3\times3$ block: $M = KR$ — upper triangular × orthogonal
  • this split is the RQ decomposition (QR "from the other side"), unique with a positive diagonal of $K$
  • then $t = K^{-1}\,p_4$ ($p_4$: last column)

Details

  • $P$ is known only up to scale (and sign): divide by $k_{33}$; if $\det M < 0$, flip the sign of $P$ first, so that $\det R = +1$
  • scipy.linalg.rq, cv2.decomposeProjectionMatrix
  • camera centre directly: $C = -M^{-1} p_4$

Calibration to play with

Calibration in practice

Zhang's method (what OpenCV does)

  • a flat checkerboard, easy to print — but one plane is degenerate for DLT
  • take 10–20 photos in different orientations; each view gives a homography (plane → image, lecture 3)
  • constraints from all views give $K$ in closed form
  • non-linear refinement: minimise the reprojection error over $K$, distortion and all board poses (Levenberg–Marquardt)

cv2.findChessboardCorners → cv2.cornerSubPix → cv2.calibrateCamera; ChArUco boards also work when partly occluded.

Algebraic vs geometric error

DLT minimises $\|Ap\|$, which has no physical meaning. What we care about is the distance in pixels between detected and reprojected points. Linear methods give the starting point; non-linear least squares finishes the job. Normalise the coordinates before DLT (Hartley): it is much more stable.

Good practice

  • tilt the board; cover the whole image, especially the corners (distortion)
  • fixed focus and zoom: refocusing changes $K$
  • check the RMS reprojection error (well below 1 px)
Part 3

Perspective-n-Point (PnP)

Where is the object?

Given: a calibrated camera ($K$ known), an object of known geometry (points $X_i$ in the object frame), their pixels $(u_i, v_i)$.

Find: the object pose $R, t$ in the camera frame — 6 DoF.

How many points do we need?
  • each point: 2 equations → 3 points (P3P)
  • 3 points: up to 4 solutions
  • a 4th point usually picks the right one

Where it is used

  • fiducial markers (ArUco, AprilTag): 4 corners → pose of the marker
  • object pose from a CAD model and detected keypoints
  • localisation in a known map; augmented reality
  • calibration: pose of the board in each view

Calibration: unknown $K$, 11 DoF, $\ge 6$ points. PnP: known $K$, 6 DoF, $\ge 3$ points.

Why up to four solutions?

  • known: the angles $\theta_{ij}$ between the rays (from the pixels and $K$) and the distances $a_{ij}$ between the points (from the model)
  • unknown: the distances $d_1, d_2, d_3$ along the rays
  • law of cosines for each pair: $$a_{ij}^2 = d_i^2 + d_j^2 - 2 d_i d_j \cos\theta_{ij}$$
  • 3 quadratic equations in 3 unknowns → one polynomial of degree 4 → up to 4 real solutions
  • given $d_i$, the 3D points in the camera frame are known; $R, t$ follow by aligning two point sets

PnP as least squares

With many points and noise, minimise the reprojection error:

$$\min_{R,\,t} \sum_i \big\|\, \pi\big(K(R X_i + t)\big) - (u_i, v_i) \,\big\|^2$$

$\pi(a, b, c) = (a/c,\ b/c)$. Non-linear: start from a P3P / EPnP solution and refine (Gauss–Newton / Levenberg–Marquardt), with $R$ parametrised by a rotation vector (lecture 1).

OpenCV

  • cv2.solvePnP(X, uv, K, dist, flags=…) → rvec, tvec
  • SOLVEPNP_ITERATIVE, SOLVEPNP_EPNP, SOLVEPNP_SQPNP
  • SOLVEPNP_IPPE_SQUARE: 4 corners of a square marker
  • cv2.solvePnPRansac: when some matches are wrong (next part)

Part 4

RANSAC

Wrong measurements

  • correspondences come from detectors and matchers; some are simply wrong (outliers): repeated texture, reflections, occlusions
  • 10–50% outliers is common for feature matches (lecture 3)
  • least squares weights every point: one far outlier can move the solution arbitrarily far

RANdom SAmple Consensus

Fischler & Bolles, 1981.

  1. pick a random minimal sample of $s$ points (line: 2, P3P: 3, DLT: 6)
  2. fit the model to the sample
  3. count the inliers: points with error below a threshold $\varepsilon$
  4. repeat $N$ times, keep the model with the most inliers
  5. refit on all its inliers (least squares)

RANSAC to play with: line fitting

How many iterations?

$w$: fraction of inliers, $s$: sample size, $p$: desired success probability (e.g. 0.99).

  • a sample is all inliers with probability $w^s$
  • all $N$ samples are contaminated: $(1 - w^s)^N$
  • we want $(1 - w^s)^N \le 1 - p$:
$$N \ge \frac{\log(1 - p)}{\log(1 - w^s)}$$

$w = 0.9$3457

  • does not depend on the number of points
  • grows fast with $s$: use minimal solvers
  • $w$ unknown: update $N$ from the best inlier ratio found so far (adaptive)
  • threshold $\varepsilon \approx 2$–$3\sigma$ of the measurement noise
Part 5

Simple stereo

Two cameras, two views

The same scene from two cameras side by side (the right one is 20 cm to the right).

Switch between the views:

  • close objects (the sphere, the box) jump a lot
  • far objects (the back wall) barely move

The shift (disparity) tells us the depth: the closer the object, the larger the shift.

Rectified stereo: the same row in both images

Definition

A stereo pair is rectified if its images look as if taken by two identical cameras (same $K$) with the same orientation, the right one shifted only along the $x$ axis of the left one, by the baseline $b$.

  • the plane through $P$ and both centres contains the baseline, so it cuts both image planes along the same row: $v_l = v_r$
  • the match of a pixel is searched along one row only
  • the two rays meet at $P$: triangulation

Rectification: warping the two images of a general camera pair (one homography each) into this form, cv2.stereoRectify. The general case (epipolar geometry): lecture 3.

Disparity and depth

Point $(x, y, z)$ in the left camera frame; the right camera sits at $(b, 0, 0)$:

$$u_l = f\frac{x}{z} + o_x, \qquad u_r = f\frac{x - b}{z} + o_x$$

Same row in both images: $v_l = v_r = v$.

The disparity:

$$d = u_l - u_r = \frac{f\,b}{z} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \frac{f\,b}{d}$$

$$x = \frac{b\,(u_l - o_x)}{d}, \qquad y = \frac{b\,(v - o_y)}{d}$$

  • depth is inversely proportional to disparity
  • disparity is proportional to the baseline $b$
  • far away: $d \to 0$; at infinity $d = 0$ (the same pixel in both images)
  • near: large $d$ — very close points may be visible in one camera only

Depth precision degrades with $z^2$

Disparity is measured to a fraction of a pixel. How does an error $\Delta d$ affect depth?

$$\Big|\frac{\partial z}{\partial d}\Big| = \frac{f b}{d^2} = \frac{z^2}{f b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta z \approx \frac{z^2}{f\,b}\,\Delta d$$

Example: $f = 600$ px, $b = 10$ cm, $\Delta d = 0.25$ px:

$z$1 m5 m10 m
$\Delta z$4 mm10 cm42 cm

For a robot

  • stereo is precise for grasping at arm's length, coarse for distant obstacles
  • more precision at range: larger baseline or focal length (narrower field of view)
  • the region of 3D positions consistent with both pixels (± the matching error) stretches along the ray with depth

Finding correspondences: block matching

  • rectified: the match of pixel $(u, v)$ lies on the same row $v$, at $u - d$ with $0 \le d \le d_{\max}$
  • compare a small window around $(u, v)$ in the left image with windows along the row in the right image
  • take the $d$ with the lowest cost ("winner takes all")

Window costs

SAD: $\sum |L - R|$  ·  SSD: $\sum (L - R)^2$

NCC (normalised cross-correlation): robust to brightness differences between the cameras.

A 2D search becomes a 1D search: this is why we rectify.

Block matching to play with

When matching fails

  • no texture (white wall): every window looks the same
  • repetitive texture (stripes, tiles): several equally good matches
  • occlusions: points seen by only one camera have no match
  • specular / transparent surfaces: appearance differs between the views
  • window size: small → noisy; large → blurred depth edges, and the window assumes a fronto-parallel surface (foreshortening)

Beyond block matching

  • semi-global matching (SGM): adds smoothness along several directions — cv2.StereoSGBM
  • active stereo: an infrared projector paints a random dot pattern, giving texture to white walls (e.g. Intel RealSense D400)
  • learned stereo: neural networks trained on large (often synthetic) datasets, e.g. RAFT-Stereo
  • other depth sensors: structured light, time-of-flight, lidar

Summary

  • $P = K[R\,|\,t]$, $3\times4$, 11 DoF; $t = -RC$
  • calibration (DLT): 2 linear equations per point, $\min\|Ap\|$ s.t. $\|p\| = 1$ → eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of $A^\top A$; RQ → $K, R$
  • in practice: planar board, Zhang's method, refine the reprojection error
  • PnP: known $K$, 6 DoF, P3P gives ≤ 4 solutions; refine by least squares
  • RANSAC: minimal samples + consensus; $N = \log(1-p)/\log(1-w^s)$
  • rectified stereo: $z = fb/d$, $\Delta z \approx z^2\Delta d/(fb)$
  • correspondence along the row: block matching; fails without texture, with repetitions and occlusions
